Glass for the IMX307

What fits the camera's 1/2.8-inch sensor, what it will look like, and how to mount it. The short version: this sensor is almost exactly the size of a Super 8 frame, so the lenses made for 8 mm and 16 mm cine cameras are its natural partners.

How big the sensor really is

The IMX307 records 1920 × 1080 pixels at 2.9 µm each, which is a 5.57 × 3.13 mm patch of silicon. That is tiny next to a photo sensor, but it sits right in the middle of the small-gauge film formats. Every lens designed to cover one of the larger rectangles below will cover this sensor.

Where the character livesA lens's vintage signature (swirl, soft edges, vignetting, glow) mostly lives near the edge of its image circle. On this sensor, a lens made for 16 mm only uses its central half, so it looks cleaner than it did on film. For the most "period" look, choose lenses made for the smallest formats: 8 mm, Super 8 and small CCTV formats, where the sensor uses more of the circle.

What each focal length does

Multiply by about 6.8 to get the full-frame equivalent. A "normal" lens for this sensor is roughly its diagonal, about 6.5 mm. That means the classic 16 mm cine focal lengths all act long here: a 25 mm is a telephoto.

Depth of field: the small sensor makes backgrounds harder to blur. As a rough guide, multiply the aperture by the same ×6.8 for full-frame-equivalent depth of field: f/1.4 here behaves like about f/9.5 on full frame, and f/0.95 like about f/6.4. Fast lenses still matter, for low light and for blur on close subjects.

Mounts and flange distances

The camera currently has a tiny M12 lens holder. Because we build the replacement mount ourselves, we choose the flange distance: the gap between the mount face and the sensor that every lens system assumes.

MountThreadFlangeNotes
C-mount1"-32 UN17.526 mm16 mm cine, CCTV, machine vision, microscope camera ports
CS-mount1"-32 UN12.526 mmCCTV; a C lens fits via a 5 mm ring, but a CS lens can't go on C
D-mount5/8"-3212.29 mmStandard 8 cine lenses; needs the shortest flange of all
M12 (S-mount)M12 × 0.5not fixedboard-camera lenses, focus by screwing in; best for ultra-wide and fisheye

Recommendation: build the mount with a short flange, at or just under 12.29 mm. Everything else then attaches with a simple spacer:

  • D-mount lenses screw in directly (or via a thin D thread insert).
  • CS lenses need a small shim (about 0.24 mm).
  • C-mount lenses use a D-to-C ring (about 5.24 mm) or a CS-to-C 5 mm ring.
Calibrate infinityGlass in front of the sensor (cover glass, IR-cut filter) shifts focus back by roughly a third of its thickness. Plan for a shim set (0.05–0.5 mm) and set the flange by focusing a lens at infinity, instead of trusting the drawing alone.

The IR-cut question

The IMX307 is a "starlight" sensor that sees well into near-infrared. The stock M12 lens or holder may carry an IR-cut filter, but cine lenses never do. Without one, colours drift towards magenta, foliage glows, and blacks lift under daylight and tungsten. Put a 650 nm IR-cut filter in the new mount, between lens and sensor, for accurate colour. It can be a small square of filter glass, or a removable one if you want infrared effects on purpose.

To checkWhether the board itself already has IR-cut glass over the sensor. Look for a faintly blue- or red-tinted window when the M12 lens is removed. The board has an unpopulated IR-cut switcher driver (GPIO 10/11), which hints that the filter lived in a lens holder.

Lens families worth hunting

Kern-Paillard Switar

C-mount · 16 mm (Bolex)

Swiss, beautifully made, gentle contrast and warm rendering. Prefer the non-"RX" versions: RX lenses were computed to look through the Bolex reflex prism and are softer wide open on other cameras, especially the shorter focal lengths.

10 mm f/1.6 · 16 mm f/1.8 · 25 mm f/1.4 · 75 mm f/1.9

Angénieux

C-mount · 16 mm

The classic French cine look, with a glowing highlight bloom wide open. The 25 mm f/0.95 is legendary and priced to match. The 10 mm retrofocus is a friendlier way in.

10 mm f/1.8 · 15 mm f/1.3 · 25 mm f/0.95 · 12–120 mm zoom

Kodak Cine Ektar

C-mount · 16 mm

Mid-century American glass. Crisp in the centre, with characterful falloff and often very affordable.

15 mm f/2.5 · 25 mm f/1.4

Schneider · Som Berthiot · Cooke

C-mount · 16 mm

Schneider Cinegon and Xenoplan are clinical and sharp. Som Berthiot Cinor lenses are soft and dreamy. Cooke Kinetal is the premium 16 mm line, with the "Cooke look" and prices to match.

D-mount primes

D-mount · Standard 8

Made for 8 mm film, so the sensor uses nearly their whole image circle, which gives the most period character per euro. Watch for slight corner shading, since Standard 8 is a little smaller than the sensor. They also cover the wide end that 16 mm lenses can't.

e.g. Switar 5.5 mm f/1.8 · 12.5 mm f/1.3 (Bolex D) · Wollensak, Elgeet, Ektar 8 mm lines

CCTV and machine vision

C / CS-mount · 1/2"–1"

Fujinon, Computar, Tamron, Kowa and Pentax/Cosmicar. Modern, sharp and cheap, often with f/0.95–f/1.4 speeds and short focal lengths (4–8 mm) that give real wide and normal views on this sensor. The vintage Cosmicar/Pentax TV lenses have a nice 80s video look. The budget "CCTV 25 mm f/1.4" and "35 mm f/1.7" lenses sold for mirrorless cameras mostly lose their famous swirl here, because the swirl lives at the edge.

Microscope ports

C-mount · trinocular

This board started life as a microscope camera, and C-mount is the standard microscope camera port. A C-mount mount also lets it drop straight onto a trinocular head (0.35×/0.5× relay adapters).

M12 board lenses

M12 · any small format

The easy route to ultra-wide and fisheye (1.8–3 mm). Pick ones rated for 1/2.7" or larger with a known IR-cut option. They're the least "cinematic", but tiny and cheap.

A sensible starting kit

  1. A fast 6–8 mm C/CS CCTV lens (Computar or Fujinon, f/1.2–1.4). This gives a normal field of view for everyday shooting and proves the mount and flange.
  2. A D-mount 12.5 mm f/1.3 or 5.5 mm f/1.8. The period look: small-format glass using most of its image circle.
  3. A 10 mm Switar or Angénieux for 68 mm-equivalent portraits with classic 16 mm rendering.
  4. A 650 nm IR-cut filter and a C-mount shim set. The unglamorous parts that make the rest look right.

Shooting notes for this camera

  • Manual iris lenses: leave the camera in AUTO with SPOT metering and set exposure with the lens's iris ring, watching the on-screen EV meter. Or use manual shutter at 1/50 or 1/48 for motion blur and control exposure with the iris.
  • Focus: the small sensor gives deep focus, but fast lenses wide open are still critical. Focus peaking is on the camera roadmap, and until then use the HDMI monitor at full size.
  • Heavy glass: anything like a 12–120 zoom needs a lens support. The thread alone won't hold it against a board-mounted sensor.
  • Dust: with no mirror box the sensor sits right behind the mount. Cap it whenever a lens is off.
